\subsection{Bias-Variance Tradeoff and Scaling Laws}

\subsubsection{Classical Perspective}
Consider a classifier over $k \in \{1, 2, \dots K\}$, trained with Mean Squared Error loss. Its test error is often attributed to the following three sources~\cite{gemanNeuralNetworksBias1992}.
\begin{align}
    \mathcal{E}_\text{total} = \mathcal{E}_\text{noise} + \mathcal{E}_\text{bias} + \mathcal{E}_\text{variance}
\end{align}
The first term is an intrinstic error term representing irreducible noise, the second term measure the systematic error the model makes, and the third term captures the variance in the model's predictions. As the capacity of a model increases, the bias decreases while the variance increases, leading to a parabolic test error curve~\cite{nealModernTakeBiasVariance2019a}. It is said that:

\begin{quote}
    The price to pay for achieving low bias is high variance. --- Geman et. al., 1992~\cite{gemanNeuralNetworksBias1992}
\end{quote}

This means that if the model's capacity is too small, it may underfit the training data and perform poorly on unseen data. If the model's capacity is too large, it may overfit to spurious patterns in the training data, resulting in low accuracy on unseen data anyways~\cite{belkinReconcilingModernMachine2019}. Textbooks even assert that ''a model with zero training error
is overfit to the training data and will typically generalize poorly``~\cite[pg. 221]{hastieElementsStatisticalLearning2009}. It was therefore common to carefully select the complexity of models to achieve the optimal balance between bias and variance.

\subsubsection{Modern Perspective}
Modern machine learning, however, regularly uses models with low or zero training error, and those models often give very accurate predictions on new data~\cite{belkinReconcilingModernMachine2019}. It was then proposed to reconcile this phenomenon and the classical bias-variance tradeoff using a ``double descent'' curve, as shown in Figure \ref{fig:belkin_double_descent}.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.6\textwidth]{img/intro/belkin_double_descent.png}
    \caption{Double Descent Curve~\cite{belkinReconcilingModernMachine2019}}
    \label{fig:belkin_double_descent}
\end{figure}

When originally proposed in 2019, this phenomenon was empirically validated on neural networks and random forests on the MNIST dataset~\cite{belkinReconcilingModernMachine2019}. But importantly, it was later empirically validated on a transformer-based large language model with up to 800M parameters~\cite{hernandezScalingLawsInterpretability2022}: we can clearly observe a double-descent curve in Figure \ref{fig:anthropic_scaling_laws}. The authors also propose the theory that the peak in test loss in the middle of the learning curve is the model memorizing the training data, while the continued descent afterwards is the model actually learning from the training data.
