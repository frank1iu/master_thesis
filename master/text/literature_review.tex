\subsection{Introduction to AdaRound}
AdaRound~\cite{nagelAdaptiveRoundingPostTraining2020a} is a novel rounding method, based on the key insight that round-to-nearest is suboptimal for post-training quantization (PTQ).

The authors' proposed method is to instead minimize the difference in task loss of the quantized model and the unquantized model. The task loss of layer $j$ of the unquantized model is $\mathcal{L}(x, y, w_j)$, and the task loss of the same layer of the quantized model $\mathcal{L}(x, y, w_j + \Delta w_j)$. Therefore, the authors' objective is the following.

\begin{align}
\underset{\Delta w_j}{\arg\min} \left(\mathcal{L}(x, y, w_j + \Delta w_j) - \mathcal{L}(x, y, w_j)\right)
\end{align}

The authors use a second order Taylor expansion to approximate the above objective.

\begin{align} \label{eq:adaround_taylor_expansion}
\mathcal{L}(x, y, w_j + \Delta w_j) - \mathcal{L}(x, y, w_j) \approx \nabla\mathcal{L}(x, y, w_j)^T \Delta w_j + \frac{1}{2} \Delta w_j^T \mathbf{H} \Delta w_j
\end{align}

Where $\mathbf{H}$ is the Hessian $\nabla^2\mathcal{L}(x, y, w_j)$. The authors then ignore the first term by asserting that the gradient $\nabla\mathcal{L}(x, y, w_j)$ is $0$ for converged pretrained models, which simplifies into the following.

\begin{align}
\mathcal{L}(x, y, w_j + \Delta w_j) - \mathcal{L}(x, y, w_j) \approx \frac{1}{2} \Delta w_j^T \mathbf{H} \Delta w_j
\end{align}

Finally, the authors formulate the optimization of weigh rounding for layer $j$ as the following Quadratic Unconstrained Binary Optimization (QUBO) problem.

\begin{align}
\underset{\Delta w_j}{\arg\min} \Delta w_j^T \mathbf{H} \Delta w_j
\end{align}

The above problem is NP-hard. To solve this efficiently, AdaRound uses the local mean squared error alongside a regularizer as a proxy. The regularizer encourages the optimization variables to converge towards either $0$ or $1$.

\begin{align} \label{eq:adaround_objective}
\arg\min_{\mathbf{V}} \left( \|\mathbf{W}x - \hat{\mathbf{W}}x\|_F^2 + f_\text{reg}(\mathbf{V}) \right)
\end{align}

The regularizer uses an annealed parameter $\beta$ and is defined as follows.

\begin{align}
f_{\text{reg}}(\mathbf{V}) = \sum_{i, j} 1 - \lvert 2h(\mathbf{V}_{i,j}) - 1\rvert^\beta
\end{align}

The above objective is then optimized over

\begin{align}
\hat{\mathbf{W}} &= \text{scale} \cdot \text{clamp} \left( \lfloor \frac{\mathbf{W}}{\text{scale}} \rfloor +h(\mathbf{V}), q_\text{min}, q_\text{max}\right), \\ 
q_\text{min} &= -2^{b-1}, \\
q_\text{max} &= 2^{b-1}-1.
\end{align}

The authors define $h$ as a rectified sigmoid as follows, although it can be any differentiable function with a range of $h(\mathbf{V}_{i,j}) \in [0, 1]$.

\begin{align}
h(\mathbf{V}_{i,j}) &= \text{clamp}\left( \sigma(\mathbf{V}_{i, j}) (\zeta - \gamma) + \gamma, 0, 1\right), \\
\zeta &= 1.1, \\
\gamma &= -0.1.
\end{align}

The use of a rectified sigmoid is justified as it has non-vanishing gradients near $0$ and $1$, and helps the learning process as they previously used a regularizer to encourage $h(\mathbf{V}_{i, j})$ to move to either $0$ or $1$.

\subsection{AdaRound Algorithm}
We implement AdaRound in Algorithm \ref{alg:adaround}.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{AdaRound}\label{alg:adaround}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require $\mathbf{W}$, $X$ (calibration data), $t$ (number of iterations), $b$ (target bit width), $\beta$ (regularization parameter)
\Ensure Quantized model $\mathbf{W}_q$, scales

\State $x_{in} \gets \textsc{CacheActivations}(\mathbf{W}, X)$ \Comment{Forward pass on calibration data to cache input activations}
\For{$w_j$ in $\mathbf{W}$}
\State $q_{\text{min}} \gets -2^{b - 1}$
\State $q_{\text{max}} \gets 2^{b - 1} - 1$
\State $\text{scales}_j \gets \frac{\max(w_j)}{q_{\text{max}}}$ \Comment{Store scale for output}
\State $\mathbf{V}_j \gets h^{-1}(w_j - \lfloor w_j \rfloor)$ \Comment{Initialize $\mathbf{V}_j$ so that it corresponds to the fraction of each weight}

\For{$i \in \{0 \ldots t\}$}
\State $\beta \gets \textsc{Anneal}(\beta)$
\State $\hat{w}_j \gets \text{clamp}\left(\lfloor \frac{w_j}{\text{scales}_j} \rfloor + h(\mathbf{V}_j), q_\text{min}, q_\text{max}\right)$
\State $\mathcal{L} \gets \|w_j x_{in} - \hat{w}_j x_{in}\|_F^2 + \sum_{i, j} \left(1 - \lvert 2h(\mathbf{V}_{i,j}) - 1\rvert^\beta\right)$
\State $\mathbf{V} \gets \mathbf{V} - \nabla \mathcal{L}$ \Comment{via autograd}
\EndFor
\State $\mathbf{W}_{Q_{i, j}} \gets \begin{cases}
    \lceil w_{i, j} \rceil, &\text{where } h(\mathbf{V}_{i,j}) \geq 0.5 \\
    \lfloor w_{i, j} \rfloor, &\text{otherwise}
\end{cases}$ \Comment{Finalize quantization}
\EndFor
\State \Return $\mathbf{W}_Q$, scales
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Limitations}
We identify two important limitations of AdaRound. First, using calibration data can cause some information to be encoded into the weight perturbation matrix. We empirically verify this by demonstrating a novel inversion method to reconstruct representations of training data in Chapter \ref{chap:adaround_inversion}. Second, the assumption in Equation \ref{eq:adaround_taylor_expansion} that the gradient of the loss function for pretrained converged models is neglibile (for which empirical evidence was not provided) is challenged by recent literature, which we review below.

\section{Edge of Stability and Central Flows}

In this section, we review a rebuttal to the zero-gradient assumption in Equation \ref{eq:adaround_taylor_expansion}. 

Consider gradient descent~\cite{cauchyMethodeGeneralePour1847}, where we compute the gradient of the loss function then try to move the model weights in the opposite direction to minimize the loss.

\begin{align}
    \theta_{t+1} &= \theta_t - \eta \nabla \mathcal{L}(\theta)
\end{align}

While one might expect weights to follow a smooth, stable path to an optimum, gradient descent with deep neural networks almost always operate in a highly dynamic, oscillatory regime described as the \textbf{Edge of Stability}~\cite{cohenGradientDescentNeural2022a}, leading to chaotic loss curves such as the follows.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.6\textwidth]{img/literature_review/kimi_k2_loss_curve.png}
    \caption{Training Loss Curve of Kimi K2~\cite{teamKimiK2Open2026}}
    \label{fig:kimi_k2_loss_curve}
\end{figure}

It can be observed that the oscillations in gradient descent can be viewed as bouncing between walls of a valley of low gradient~\cite{xingWalkSGD2018}. \textbf{Central Flows}~\cite{cohenUnderstandingOptimizationDeep2025a} are differential equations that attempts to model the time-averaged trajectory of an oscillatory optimizer, illustrated as follows.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.6\textwidth]{img/literature_review/central_flows_valley.png}
    \caption{Oscillatory Optimizer Bouncing between Walls in a Valley~\cite{cohenUnderstandingOptimizationDeep2025a}}
    \label{fig:central_flows_valley}
\end{figure}

The oscillatory optimization also implies that the gradient for pretrained models are rarely zero. The authors empirically show that even as the training loss decreases, the gradient of a model almost stays constant, as seen in Figure \ref{fig:central_flows_gradient_norm}.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{img/literature_review/central_flows_gradient_norm.png}
    \caption{Gradient Norms in Vision Transformer Trained on CIFAR10~\cite[pg. 15]{cohenUnderstandingOptimizationDeep2025a}}
    \label{fig:central_flows_gradient_norm}
\end{figure}

This observation creates a conflict with AdaRound's assumption in Equation \ref{eq:adaround_taylor_expansion} that the gradient for converged pretrained models is zero, which we attempt to reconciliate in Chapter \ref{chap:gradient_aware_adaround}.
