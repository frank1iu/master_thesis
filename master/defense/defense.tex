\title{Gradient-Aware Adaptive Rounding for Post-Training Quantization of \\ Deep Neural Networks \vspace{-1.5em}}
\author{48246347 Piye Liu \\ Supervisor: Prof. Hiroshi Nakamura}
\date{}

\frame{\vspace{-2em} \titlepage \vspace{-4em} \scriptsize 
The recent exponential scaling of Large Language Models (LLMs) has resulted in massive carbon emissions and significant challenges in deployment, forcing users to rely on third-party cloud providers, thereby raising privacy concerns. This highlights the necessity of model compression, and post-training quantization (PTQ) is one method that enables efficient on-device models. AdaRound, an adaptive rounding method that outperforms round-to-nearest rounding during quantization, minimizes the task loss between the original weights and the quantized weights on unlabelled calibration data. We find that: 1) AdaRound leaks some information about its calibration data into the weight perturbation matrix, and we demonstrate this by proposing a novel inversion attack that targets the null space of this matrix to recover coarse representations of the original data. 2) The theoretical foundation of AdaRound asserts that the gradient of the loss function is zero for pretrained converged models, an assumption which is refuted by recent literature. To address this discrepancy, we attempted to reconcile this by incorporating local gradient signals into the AdaRound optimization process, but obtained neutral results on empirical evaluations. Therefore, this thesis highlights a frequently overlooked dimension in PTQ (privacy), and proposes a gradient-aware modification of AdaRound to better align the method with modern literature.
}

\begin{frame}{Problems with Third-Party APIs}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Environmental Effects:} GPT-3's training had an estimated $\text{CO}_2e$ of $\sim$552.1 tons~\cite{pattersonCarbonEmissionsLarge2021}, GPT-4's training had an estimated $\text{CO}_2e$ of $\sim$6912 tons~\cite{kausarQuantifyingClimateRisk2026}, and Llama 3.3~\cite{grattafioriLlama3Herd2024c}'s training had an estimated $\text{CO}_2e$ of $\sim$11,390 tons.
    \item \textbf{Cost:} For example, OpenAI o1~\cite{openaiOpenAIO1System2026} in ``pro mode'' costs \$150 per 1 million tokens input, and \$600 per 1 million tokens output. Uber's yearly AI budget was exhausted in 4 months, while Microsoft began cancelling Claude Code licenses just 6 months after acquiring them~\cite{jakeUberBurnedIts}.
    \item \textbf{Reliability:} Single point of failure, and an outage can have severe effects on mission-critical tasks. For example, it is observed that whenever ChatGPT has an outage, stock trading volume goes down~\cite{chengDoesGenerativeAI2025}.
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}
\begin{frame}{Benefits of On-Device Inference}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Privacy:} Data does not need to leave the device
    \item \textbf{Robustness:} Can function even without internet access
    \item \textbf{Latency:} Doesn't have to rely on third-party scheduling, can prioritize urgent tasks
    \item \textbf{Cost:} Reduces API fees, network costs, and even environmental emissions
  \end{itemize}
  Therefore, our goal is to improve model compression to enable efficient on-device AI. Furthermore, we also explore a side effect of model compression (privacy): data-dependent post-training quantization may leak some calibration data. 
\end{frame}
\begin{frame}{Model Compression Methods}
  Methods typically used for the compression of deep neural networks:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Weight Pruning: Setting some weights to zeroes, resulting in sparse weight matrices~\cite{wangStructuredPruningLarge2020a}.
    \item Knowledge Distillation: Smaller model learns to match output logits of larger model~\cite{hintonDistillingKnowledgeNeural2015c}.
    \item Weight Palettization: Clusters weights (e.g. via $k$-means), then stores every weight as the centroid of the cluster they belong to~\cite{wuDeep$k$MeansReTraining2018a}.
    \item Quantization: \textbf{We focus on this method for our thesis.} Stores weights in a data type of less bit width (e.g. int8), compared to the original data type (e.g. float32)~\cite{jacobQuantizationTrainingNeural2017a}.
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}
\begin{frame}{Objective \& Contributions}
  Overall, our goal is to investigate model compression and its side effects (specifically, privacy) to enable efficient on-device AI. We achieve this by the following.
  \begin{itemize}
    \item A novel inversion method against AdaRound to recover coarse outlines that represent its calibration data.
    \item A proposed gradient-aware adaptive rounding method based on AdaRound, applied on two language models.
  \end{itemize} 
\end{frame}

\section{Preliminaries}
\begin{frame}{Compressibility of Deep Neural Networks}
Source coding theorem~\cite{shannonInformationTheory}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $n$ i.i.d. (independent and identically distributed) random variables, each with entropy $H(X)$, can be compressed to $nH(X)$ bits on average, with negligible information loss, as $n \rightarrow \infty$ 
\end{itemize}
In this case, $H(X)$, the entropy of a random variable, is the expected amount of information needed to describe it, and can be computed by:
\begin{align}
H(X)&:=-\sum_{x\in X}p(x)\log_2 p(x).
\end{align}
Note that the log base determines the units: $\log_2$ yields bits, $\log_{10}$ yields dits, and $\ln$ yields nats.
\end{frame}
\begin{frame}{Measuring Compressibility of LLMs}
Total information stored in LLM weights: $\sim n \cdot \hat{H}(W)$ bits; Maximum possible total entropy of the model: $n\cdot b$ bits. It therefore follows that the capacity utilization by a model is:
\begin{align}
U(W) &:= \frac{\hat{H}(W)}{b}.
\end{align}
We use this method to quantify the capacity utilization of several modern LLMs:

\begin{frame}{Evaluation of Our LMs}
We use perplexity~\cite{jelinekPerplexityMeasureDifficulty1977} as a quantitative metric to evaluate a language model's accuracy. For a model $\theta$, a text $x$ of length $N$, it is defined as the following.
\begin{align}
    \text{Perplexity}(\theta, x) = \left( \prod_{i=1}^N \frac{1}{p_\theta(x_i\mid x_1, x_2, \dots, x_{i-1})} \right)^{\frac1N}
\end{align}
In other words, it is the geometric mean of the inverse probabilities of a given text.

\begin{frame}{Baseline Round-to-Nearest Quantization}
Consider the symmetric uniform quantization method to int8:
  \begin{itemize}
      \item Quantization:
      \begin{itemize}
      \item $\text{scale} = \frac{\max(|W|)}{2^7-1}$
      \item $W_{\text{quantized}} = \text{clamp}(\lfloor\frac{W}{\text{scale}}\rceil)$ 
  \end{itemize}
      \item Reconstruction (dequantization):
      \begin{itemize}
          \item $W_\text{reconstructed}=\text{scale}\cdot W_\text{quantized}$
      \end{itemize}
      \item Note that clamp() here refers to clamping a number between $[-2^7,2^7-1]$ to ensure its within the range of the data type.
  \end{itemize}
  Evidently, model weights are perturbed in the direction of $W_\text{reconstructed} - W$ after quantization and dequantization. We investigate the effects of perturbation direction on model accuracy on our TinyStories V2 model.
\end{frame}

\section{Literature Review}
\begin{frame}{AdaRound: Objective}
AdaRound~\cite{nagelAdaptiveRoundingPostTraining2020a} is a novel rounding method, which aims to minimize the difference in task loss of the quantized model and the unquantized model. The task loss of layer $j$ of the unquantized model is $\mathcal{L}(x, y, w_j)$, and the task loss of the same layer of the quantized model $\mathcal{L}(x, y, w_j + \Delta w_j)$. Therefore, the objective is:
\begin{align}
\underset{\Delta w_j}{\arg\min} \left(\mathcal{L}(x, y, w_j + \Delta w_j) - \mathcal{L}(x, y, w_j)\right).
\end{align}
Using second order Taylor expansion:
\begin{align} \label{eq:adaround_taylor_expansion}
\mathcal{L}(x, y, w_j + \Delta w_j) - \mathcal{L}(x, y, w_j) \approx \nabla\mathcal{L}(x, y, w_j)^T \Delta w_j + \frac{1}{2} \Delta w_j^T \mathbf{H} \Delta w_j
\end{align}
\end{frame}
\begin{frame}{AdaRound: Approximations}
The authors assert that $\nabla\mathcal{L}(x, y, w_j)$ is negligible, which transforms the objective into just:
\begin{align}
\mathcal{L}(x, y, w_j + \Delta w_j) - \mathcal{L}(x, y, w_j) \approx \frac{1}{2} \Delta w_j^T \mathbf{H} \Delta w_j
\end{align}
Finally, the authors formulate the optimization of weight rounding for layer $j$ as the following Quadratic Unconstrained Binary Optimization (QUBO) problem.
\begin{align}
\underset{\Delta w_j}{\arg\min} \Delta w_j^T \mathbf{H} \Delta w_j
\end{align}
The above problem is NP-hard, so AdaRound uses the local mean squared error alongside a regularizer as a proxy. 

\begin{align} \label{eq:adaround_objective}
\arg\min_{\mathbf{V}} \left( \|\mathbf{W}x - \hat{\mathbf{W}}x\|_F^2 + f_\text{reg}(\mathbf{V}) \right)
\end{align}
\end{frame}
\begin{frame}{AdaRound: Algorithm}
\begin{algorithm}[H]
\scriptsize
\caption{AdaRound}\label{alg:adaround}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require $\mathbf{W}$, $X$ (calibration data), $t$ (number of iterations), $b$ (target bit width), $\beta$ (regularization parameter)
\Ensure Quantized model $\mathbf{W}_q$, scales

\State $x_{in} \gets \textsc{CacheActivations}(\mathbf{W}, X)$ \Comment{Forward pass on calibration data to cache input activations}
\For{$w_j$ in $\mathbf{W}$}
\State $q_{\text{min}} \gets -2^{b - 1}$
\State $q_{\text{max}} \gets 2^{b - 1} - 1$
\State $\text{scales}_j \gets \frac{\max(w_j)}{q_{\text{max}}}$ \Comment{Store scale for output}
\State $\mathbf{V}_j \gets h^{-1}(w_j - \lfloor w_j \rfloor)$ \Comment{Initialize $\mathbf{V}_j$}

\For{$i \in \{0 \ldots t\}$}
\State $\beta \gets \textsc{Anneal}(\beta)$
\State $\hat{w}_j \gets \text{clamp}\left(\lfloor \frac{w_j}{\text{scales}_j} \rfloor + h(\mathbf{V}_j), q_\text{min}, q_\text{max}\right)$
\State $\mathcal{L} \gets \|w_j x_{in} - \hat{w}_j x_{in}\|_F^2 + \sum_{i, j} \left(1 - \lvert 2h(\mathbf{V}_{i,j}) - 1\rvert^\beta\right)$
\State $\mathbf{V} \gets \mathbf{V} - \nabla \mathcal{L}$ \Comment{via autograd}
\EndFor
\State $\mathbf{W}_{Q_{i, j}} \gets \begin{cases}
    \lceil w_{i, j} \rceil, &\text{where } h(\mathbf{V}_{i,j}) \geq 0.5 \\
    \lfloor w_{i, j} \rfloor, &\text{otherwise}
\end{cases}$ \Comment{Finalize quantization}
\EndFor
\State \Return $\mathbf{W}_Q$, scales
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}
\end{frame}
\begin{frame}{Limitations of AdaRound}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Privacy} of calibration data: AdaRound can cause some information to be encoded into the weight perturbation matrix. We empirically verify this in Contribution 1.
  \item The assumption in Equation \ref{eq:adaround_taylor_expansion} that the gradient of the loss function for pretrained converged models is negligible (for which empirical evidence was not provided) is \textbf{challenged by recent literature.} 
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}
\begin{frame}{AdaRound's Zero-Gradient Assumption}
While one might expect gradient descent to follow a smooth, stable path to an optimum, in reality it almost always operate in a highly dynamic, oscillatory regime described as the \textbf{Edge of Stability}~\cite{cohenGradientDescentNeural2022a}, leading to chaotic loss curves such as:

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.4\textwidth]{img/literature_review/kimi_k2_loss_curve.png}
    \caption{Training Loss Curve of Kimi K2~\cite{teamKimiK2Open2026}}
    \label{fig:kimi_k2_loss_curve}
\end{figure}
The oscillatory optimization also implies that the gradient for pretrained models are rarely zero. Recent literature~\cite{cohenGradientDescentNeural2022a} empirically show that even as the training loss decreases, the gradient norm of a model almost stays constant.
\end{frame}

\section{Contribution 1: Inversion Attack on AdaRound}
\begin{frame}{Leakage Channel: The Null Space}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item AdaRound's objective is to make $\|Wx - \hat{W}x\|_F^2$ approach zero. Let $\Delta W = W - \hat{W}$. For a model quantized using AdaRound:
    \item[] {\Large
    \begin{align*}
        \Delta W x &\approx 0
    \end{align*}
    }
    \item So the calibration data $x$ is approximately in the \textbf{null space} of the perturbation matrix. In other words, $x \subset \mathcal{N}(\Delta W)$. We can therefore exploit this property to generate samples that resemble the mean of the calibration data.
    \item We \textbf{can't} reconstruct exact pixel-for-pixel calibration samples. However, we demonstrate that by implementing this as a simple optimization problem, we can reconstruct representations that look like the mean of these calibration samples.
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}
\begin{frame}{Realizing an Attack}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item If an attacker has access to both the original weights ($W$) and the quantized weights ($\hat{W}$), they can recover representations of the calibration data by searching for any vector that satisfies this null space property.
    \item For example, hypothetically: a company downloads an open-source vision model, quantizes it with AdaRound using confidential internal data (e.g. patient records), then deploys it onto edge devices sold to customers. An attacker can reconstruct representations of the calibration data by extracting the quantized $\hat{W}$ from the edge devices, then performing the inversion on the weight perturbation matrix $\Delta W = W - \hat{W}$.
  \end{itemize}
  
\end{frame}
\begin{frame}{Proposed Inversion Algorithm}

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{AdaRound Inversion Algorithm}\label{alg:inversion}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require $W$, $\hat{W}$, $\hat{x}_{\text{init}}$, No. of Iterations $K$, Learning Rate $\eta$
\State $\Delta W \gets W - \hat{W}$
\State $\hat{x} \gets \hat{x}_\text{init}$
\For{$k = 1$ \textbf{to} $K$}
    \State $\mathcal{L}  \gets \sum \left( \Delta W  \tanh(\hat{x}) \right)^2$ (Forward pass)
    \State $\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \hat{x}} \gets \text{Autograd}(\mathcal{L}, \hat{x})$ (Backward pass)
    \State $\hat{x} \gets \hat{x} - \eta \frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial{\hat{x}}}$ (Update $\hat{x}$)
\EndFor
\State \Return $\tanh(\hat{x})$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}
\end{frame}
\begin{frame}{Simple Optimization}
Note that the objective can be rewritten as:
\begin{align}
\|\Delta W \hat{x}\|_2^2 = (\Delta W \hat{x})^T(\Delta W \hat{x}) = \hat{x}^T (\Delta W^T \Delta W) \hat{x}.
\end{align}We can therefore make this simple optimization:

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Optimized AdaRound Inversion Algorithm}\label{alg:inversion_optimized}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require $W$, $\hat{W}$, $\hat{x}_{\text{init}}$, No. of Iterations $K$, Learning Rate $\eta$
\State $\Delta W \gets W - \hat{W}$
\State $\hat{x} \gets \hat{x}_\text{init}$
\State $G \gets \Delta W^T \Delta W$
\For{$k = 1$ \textbf{to} $K$}
    \State $\mathcal{L}  \gets \sum \left( \tanh(\hat{x})^TG  \tanh(\hat{x}) \right)$ (Forward pass)
    \State $\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \hat{x}} \gets \text{Autograd}(\mathcal{L}, \hat{x})$ (Backward pass)
    \State $\hat{x} \gets \hat{x} - \eta \frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial{\hat{x}}}$ (Update $\hat{x}$)
\EndFor
\State \Return $\tanh(\hat{x})$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}
\end{frame}

\section{Contribution 2: Gradient-Aware Adaptive Rounding}
\begin{frame}{Method}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item To reconcile AdaRound's assumption that the gradient term in Equation \ref{eq:adaround_taylor_expansion} can be ignored, and Central Flows' evidence that the gradient is nonzero even for pretrained converged models, we propose a modified version of AdaRound to incorporate awareness of the local gradient.
    \item In order to minimize
\begin{align}
    \mathbb{E} \left[ g^{(w)^T} \Delta W \right],
\end{align}
we can attempt to force $\Delta W$ to have the same sign as $-g^{(w)^T}$. 
\item We do this by finetuning the model on a small amount of data then taking the EMA of the model during finetuning as $W'_{\text{avg}}$. We add the Binary Cross Entropy (BCE) between the current rounding directions and $\text{sign}(W'_{\text{avg}} - W)$ as a penalty term to AdaRound's optimization objective. 
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}
\begin{frame}{Algorithm for EMA Finetuning}
\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Gradient-Aware Adaptive Rounding (Finetuning)}\label{alg:gradient_aware_adaround_finetuning}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require $\mathbf{W}$, $X$ (calibration data), $\eta$ (learning rate), $B$ (batch size)
\Ensure EMA of models during finetuning $W'_{\text{avg}}$
\State $\mathbf{W}' \gets \mathbf{W}$
\State $\mathbf{W}'_{\text{avg}} \gets \mathbf{W}$
\State $N \gets \frac{X}{B}$ \Comment{Number of Iterations}
\State $\alpha \gets \frac{2}{N+1}$ \Comment{EMA Smoothing Factor}
\For{each batch $x_b$ in $X$}
    \State $\hat{x}_b \gets \mathbf{W}'(x_b)$ \Comment{Forward pass}
    \State $\mathcal{L} \gets \text{CrossEntropyLoss}(\hat{x}_b, x_b)$
    \State $\mathbf{W}' \gets \mathbf{W}' - \eta \cdot \nabla \mathcal{L}$ \Comment{Backward pass}
    \State $\mathbf{W}'_{\text{avg}} \gets \alpha \cdot \mathbf{W}'_{\text{avg}} + (1 - \alpha) \cdot \mathbf{W}'$ \Comment{Update EMA}
\EndFor
\State \Return $\mathbf{W}'_{\text{avg}}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}
\end{frame}
\begin{frame}{Proposed Method Objective}
Our proposed method is essentially a modification to AdaRound's objective in Equation \ref{eq:adaround_objective}:

\begin{align}
\arg\min_{\mathbf{V}} \left( \|\mathbf{W}x - \hat{\mathbf{W}}x\|_F^2 + f_\text{reg}(\mathbf{V}) + \underbrace{\psi \cdot \textsc{BCE}\left(h(\mathbf{V}_j), \text{sign}(\mathbf{W}' - \mathbf{W})\right)}_{\text{Proposed addition}} \right)
\end{align}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Ablation Study: Method}
Note that ablating AdaRound means we steer the rounding using only the sign of the weight perturbation matrix:
\begin{algorithm}[H] \small
\caption{Gradient-based Steering without AdaRound}\label{alg:ablate_adaround}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require $\mathbf{W}$ (unquantized weights), $\mathbf{W}'$ (calibration model), $b$ (target bit width)
\Ensure Quantized model $\mathbf{W}_q$
\Function{CustomRound}{$\mathbf{W}$, $\mathbf{W}'$}
  \State $\mathbf{W}_{i,j} \gets \begin{cases}
    \lceil \mathbf{W}_{i, j} \rceil, &\text{where } \mathbf{W}_{i, j} < \mathbf{W}'_{i, j} \\
    \lfloor \mathbf{W}_{i, j} \rfloor, &\text{otherwise}
\end{cases}$
  \State \Return $\mathbf{W}$
\EndFunction
\State $q_{\text{min}} \gets -2^{b - 1}$
\State $q_{\text{max}} \gets 2^{b - 1} - 1$
\State $\text{scale} \gets \frac{\max(\mathbf{W})}{q_{\text{max}}}$
\State $\mathbf{W}_q \gets \text{clamp}\left(\textsc{CustomRound}(\frac{\mathbf{W}}{\text{scale}}), q_\text{min}, q_\text{max}\right)$
\State \Return $\mathbf{W}_q$, scale
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Discussion}
  \begin{itemize}
    \item We notice that we achieved the best performance by ablating both EMA and AdaRound (outperforming original AdaRound)
    \item This ablation study shows that the gradient direction can still potentially be useful despite the neutral to negative results in the main experiment.
    \item However, it is also possible that while the gradient is non-zero, the assertion that it is zero is simply ``good enough'' for the search of the optimal rounding direction.
    \item Finally, it might be possible to consider a better proxy than the direction towards a finetuned model.
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\section{Conclusion}
\begin{frame}{Conclusion}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{AdaRound Inversion:} We found that AdaRound inadvertently encodes information about its calibration data into its weight perturbation matrix. We propose a novel inversion method to reconstruct representations of the calibration data and empirically confirmed the existance of the leakage channel.
  \item \textbf{Gradient-Aware AdaRound:} We challenged an assertion in AdaRound that the gradient of the loss function for pretrained converged models is negligible. However, we obtained neutral to negative results using our proposed method. An ablation study actually outperformed AdaRound, potentially implying that the gradient for pretrained converged can still be a useful signal.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}
\begin{frame}{Future Work}
In the future, we would like to:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Improve the quality of the inversion attack using regularizers (e.g. Total Variation (TV) loss) and priors (e.g. deep image priors). 
    \item Furthermore explore the incorporation of the gradient of the loss function in AdaRound, possibly using other proxies to estimate the direction of the gradient. \item Replicate our experiments on models of larger scale, so we can determine whether its behavior persists beyond smaller on-device models.
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}
